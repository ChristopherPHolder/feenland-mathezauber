# Feenland Mathezauber

A touch-first math adventure for children, with responsive phone, tablet and desktop layouts. The user-approved reference is a fairyland with a blonde, blue-eyed fairy in a pale mint leaf dress and gold slippers, cream surfaces, dark blue text and pink primary actions. Keep artwork simple in the first delivery.

The Home screen offers Continue/Start, an All games button, one card per game (castle, crocodile, mushroom drums, firework show, magic walls, unicorn race, treasure lock, potion cauldron, star catcher, missing-number stages) with its own Play button, cumulative achievements and a Settings gear. The games hub lists every game and every level as a card that starts play with one tap; nothing is ever locked, a child may pick any game or level at any time, and progress only adds stars, best scores and a "Your next step" badge. Launch languages are en/de/es/fr; language selection lives in Settings. Content follows World → Chapter → Level → Challenge with stable IDs; a chapter is a game. Progress stays locally and earned accomplishments survive catalog growth. The old berry and number-bridge content is removed by explicit user request; the unicorn returned in November 2026 as the Unicorn Rainbow Race.

Current delivery includes the twenty-wave Fairy Tower Defense campaign, plus thirty selectable math stages in six topics (tens and ones, adding, taking away, times tables, sharing and mixed magic), ordered easiest first from making ten and sums to 10 up to every times table, halving, dividing within the tables and missing numbers to 100. In German and French the division sign shows as a colon (35 : 5), the way those schools write it. Each stage is a battle of ten questions, about two to three minutes. Easy times tables stay in the ×2, ×5 and ×10 rows with products up to 20; hard ones leave out ×2. The game start screen offers the castle and every stage as large picture cards grouped under a heading per topic showing the stage number, example equation, skill and range, tapping a card starts that game right away, and every wave is open from the start so a child can tap straight into any of them. Difficulty and pace are independent remembered preferences. Real practice completion is saved separately from demos. Existing campaign saves remain valid.

The Numbers world also offers The Hungry Crocodile, a seventeen-level comparison game built around the classroom trick that the crocodile's open mouth always eats the bigger number. Levels sit in five topics and move from comparing numbers up to 10, 20, 100 and 1000, to adding or subtracting before comparing (first up to 10, then 20), to two calculations on either side, mixed numbers and sums up to 100 and two calculations up to 100, and finally times tables: the 2, 5 and 10 rows against a number, facts with products up to 30 (like 4 × 5 ○ 3 × 7), where Kiki can earn a magic top hat, and facts up to 100, and finally sharing: half of an even number against a number (½ of 16 ○ 7), sharing by 2, 5 and 10, and times facts against sharing facts (4 × 3 ○ 24 ÷ 2). A round feeds the crocodile fifteen snacks when the child only compares numbers, twelve with one calculation and ten with two, so every level lasts about two to three minutes; the seven original levels each hide one of Kiki's hats; a wrong pick lets Kiki shake her head and hold up a picture rule card for the retry ("Kiki always opens her mouth toward the bigger side", "work out each side first", "look at the tens first", "count up", "count in equal steps"), chosen by level and number of tries and never by the answer, so help never shows a worked-out value, a bar sized to a value, or a hint that the sides are equal, every level is open from the start, stars reward a careful round, and finishing always counts. Rounds play out in Kiki’s pond: every snack scores points, three first-try answers in a row double them and six triple them in a feeding frenzy, two golden snacks per round and the big fish at the end pay extra, and the best score per level is remembered. Two stars in a level add a hat to Kiki’s wardrobe, which she wears from then on. Hovering or focusing an answer makes Kiki turn and open her jaws toward that side before the child commits, the number tiles bob on the water and get gulped when eaten, and a frog, butterfly and lily pads live in the pond.

Addition Island offers Magic walls, an eleven-level number wall (Rechenmauer) game in three topics (two-brick walls, three rows and four rows, from tiny walls up to 10 to a four-row giant wall up to 100), with eight small walls, five or six three-row walls or four tall walls per level so each lasts two to four minutes, in which two bricks side by side always make the brick above them. The intro shows the illustrated fairy and a tiny demo wall, every level is open from the start with stars rewarding a finished level, a plus sign marks the pair of bricks that belongs to the glowing brick, and help after a miss names the rule but never the result: the first miss is a gentle retry that already says which two bricks go together ("3 + ? = 6"), and from the second miss a tiny picture of the rule sits beside the trick while the two bricks to add hop and get a green outline on the wall. There is no number line and no button that fills the brick in; the child keeps working the brick out, however many tries it takes. The scene is alive: clouds and a bird drift across the sky, the two bricks to add pulse together, a placed brick drops in with a puff of dust while the illustrated fairy from the intro swoops down and taps it with her wand, the troll (a big-nosed green troll with one tooth and a club on his shoulder) marches down the road swinging his legs, grows and kicks up dust as he nears the wall, frowns and tints the sky when he is close, brings his club down on the wall at a bonk, shuts his eyes and turns frosty under fairy dust, and does a victory hop when the wall tumbles, and gems fly up when a wall is built. Finishing a level shows a celebration that fills the screen: the stars pop in one by one above the illustrated fairy (who dances with confetti for three stars), then reward tiles pop up for the gems won, the bricks right on the first try out of all bricks, the best streak and, with a troll, how many walls he knocked down (a tiny troll hops on that tile when he got one, or sleeps when he did not), followed by Next level (or Play again on the last level), Choose a level and All games.

The crocodile intro offers four large illustrated pace choices: No rush (the initial default), Relaxed (30 seconds per number snack / 45 for calculations), Normal (15 / 25), and Fast (8 / 15). The selected pace, including No rush, is remembered locally across visits and levels; unavailable or invalid storage falls back safely. The pace is the speed of a greedy heron rather than a visible clock: with any pace but No rush, the heron flies in across the pond toward the snack, feeding Kiki first shoos it away with up to 20 bonus points by distance, and letting it land snatches the snack, resets the combo, counts as one mistake and moves the round on. The heron pauses in a hidden tab, a child can send it away during play, and the heron rounds keep their own best score. The lily pads and Kiki can be tapped directly to answer. All four languages include the challenge.

The Tens world also offers the Unicorn Rainbow Race, a fluency game of twenty-two tracks in five cups (four in the Meadow Cup, five in the Sky, Starlight and Castle Cups, three in the Rainbow Cup), each track run in six heats, in the spirit of racing maths mini-games: Lumi the unicorn gallops to the rainbow castle without stopping. Four shuffled answers sit under the sum; a right answer gives Lumi a sprint and adds to her cruising speed so a streak snowballs, a wrong one strikes that choice out, makes her stumble and halves her pace, and three in a row is a rainbow dash worth more sparkles. Every track is run in six heats that get longer and quicker and turn from day to dusk to night: a warm-up race, a key chase, a shadow run, a big race, a night chase with a longer head start, and a cup final that must be won. A race is against three friends (Cloud the pegasus, Minty the unicorn and Peach the pony); in a chase Storm the pegasus has grabbed the castle key and a head start and must be passed before the castle; in a shadow run Lumi races her own shadow, which runs the par time for that heat until the child sets a faster best. The intro shows the six heats of the chosen track with their cups and best times, a track opens on its first unfinished heat, the finish offers the next heat straight away, and race cards show how many of the six heats are done. The Meadow Cup covers bonds to ten, adding to 10, taking away within 10 and plus and minus to 20; the Sky Cup doubles and near doubles, halves up to 100, plus and minus across ten, plus and minus to 100 and two-digit sums without carrying; the Starlight Cup the 2, 5 and 10 times tables, the 3 and 4 times tables, sharing by 2, 5 and 10, missing factors and the 6 to 9 times tables; the Castle Cup missing numbers, the hard times tables up to 30, two-digit sums across a ten, sharing up to 100 by 3 to 9 and a mixed final that only a win masters; and the Rainbow Cup mixes everything in a chase, a shadow race and a Grand Prix final that must also be won. Each cup has its own scenery (a flower meadow, a path above the clouds, a starry night, a sunset with lanterns and a pink sky with rainbows arching over the track) and page colours. Finishing three races of a cup earns a keepsake Lumi can wear in every race and in the intro line-up (a flower wreath, cloud wings, a star tiara, the castle crown and a rainbow cape), chosen in a Dress Lumi panel; a new keepsake is put on straight away. During a race the streak chip shows three pips filling toward the rainbow dash, the last fifth of the track calls the final stretch, the camera widens with speed, and a win sets off fireworks over the castle. The scene reacts to every answer: a right one bursts sparkles and speed lines around Lumi, a wrong one drops her head, kicks up dust, spins dizzy stars over her and shakes the camera; the ponies paw the ground before the start, flap their wings, swish their tails and nod as they run, and the winner rears up on the line. Race cards show the stars earned, the cup and the best time. The intro offers three remembered rival paces (Relaxed, Normal and Fast) that set how fast the others run; the shadow ignores the pace. Tracks are tuned so a race takes about a minute to a minute and a half of steady answering (fourteen to twenty questions), with later cups a little longer, and at Normal pace a child who knows the facts wins about half the time. Finishing always counts; first place earns three stars and a gold cup, a close second (within a few seconds of Storm or within a quarter of the shadow's time) two stars and silver, and every other finish one star and bronze. Best times, cups and sparkles stay on the device, the intro shows how many of the four cups are complete, the finish shows confetti, the stars and a podium line with Next race, Race again, Choose a race, the celebration and All games, and all four languages include every race. A wrong answer never reveals the result: the child keeps working the same sum out.

The Tens world also offers The Treasure Lock (since November 2026), a mental-strategy puzzle game in the spirit of the research-backed number-sense games where every move acts directly on numbers. Pip the firefly sits on a magic dial of numbers and a treasure chest shows its keyhole number; the child taps hop spells (+5, +1, −1, +3, +10, −10, ×2, ÷2) to move Pip and must land exactly on the keyhole. Every lock has a fewest-hops answer (found by the game's solver), shown only as a goal ("3 hops for three keys"), so the child plans the way and discovers the trick rather than being shown it. Nine levels each teach one mental maths trick, explained on a trick card on the intro: hops of five with a +1/−1 fix (dial to 20), threes and twos (making numbers from parts), tens then ones (place value to 50), the nine trick (+10 −1, to 50, with a thorn), hopping back from 50, doubling chains from 1 (to 40), halving back from 40 (÷2 works only on even numbers; on an odd one the button greys out and says why), a treasure master level mixing tens, doubles and steps on a dial to 100 with two thorns, and double and halve on a dial to 100. A round is six chests, easier first, ending in a big treasure chest that needs the most hops and pays double. A chest opened in the fewest hops wins three golden keys, up to two extra hops two keys, any opening one key; three perfect chests in a row start the treasure fever and double coins; a gem lying on one of the shortest ways pays extra; thorny numbers bounce Pip back and still cost the hop. Extra hops never fail a lock: after two extra hops Pip reminds the child of the level's trick (never a value), and Back to the start is always free. The intro offers a remembered hourglass choice (No hourglass, Relaxed, Normal, Fast) whose time grows with the lock's hops; with an hourglass each chest carries a crystal worth 15 coins that fades if the sand runs out, while the chest still opens. The dial is a 3D stone ring of number tiles with a hopping, glowing Pip, a pulsing gold keyhole, spinning gems, thorn bushes and a chest whose lid flies open in a burst of coins, with the flat SVG dial as its fallback. Round stars come from the keys (17 of 18 earns three, 12 earns two), the best coins per level are remembered, every opened chest warms the dragon egg, and all four languages include the game.

The Number Kingdom also offers The Mushroom Drums (since November 2026), a rhythm game for skip counting and counting on and back from any number, the bridge from counting to the times tables and the base of "add 10, take 10" mental maths. Pom the hedgehog bounces along a path of glowing drum-mushrooms in a twilight forest, and every mushroom is the next number in a pattern; the child taps the next number from three or four big buttons before Pom lands. Twelve levels in five topics run from tens, fives and twos, through tens from any number, odd twos and fives off the tens, to counting back (tens, twos and fives, ones and tens across a ten), the three and four times-table steps and a drum master level past 100. A round is three verses and a drum solo: Pom first hops two steps by himself to show the pattern (skippable), verse 2 starts somewhere awkward or turns round, verse 3 is call and response with fresh starts and a "what comes before?" break, and the solo's four drums pay double. The intro offers a remembered tempo (Free play with no beat, the default; Slow, Medium and Quick at about 3.2, 2.5 and 2 seconds a drum) and an optional tap-along that widens the on-beat window for late headphones. With a beat, an early answer lands on the beat, an answer up to 0.4 s after the landing still counts as on the beat, and a late one is never a fail: Pom waits on the edge with a drumroll and the song loses an instrument; a wrong tap holds the beat on a chord until the right one. Everything is scheduled on the audio clock, and every sound has a visual twin: four beat dots, pulsing mushrooms and a countdown ring on the "?". Four first-try answers in a row add an instrument (beetle drums, badger tuba, mouse flute, moth bells, frog choir) and the whole band lights up the forest. Help never gives the answer: after a miss Pom names the step, after a second a rule card shows the rule with other numbers, and later misses grey out one wrong button at a time, always leaving two. The wrong buttons are real mistakes (the next number, a skipped or repeated ten, the wrong way, German digit swaps and French "soixante-dix" slips). A hundred chart beside the path lights each landing, so tens fall down a column and fives make two; each round's chart is kept in a pattern gallery on the intro. The level card shows a short film of Pom hopping and a finger tapping the next number, the answer buttons hop together for a child's first five drums and after six idle seconds, and the "?" button reopens the film. The 3D forest has a flat SVG path as its fallback, stars come from first tries (32 of 36 for three), the best points per level and tempo are remembered, every right answer warms the dragon egg, and all four languages include the game.

The Tens world also offers The Potion Cauldron (since November 2026), a flexible-arithmetic puzzle in the spirit of the classic make-a-number card games teachers use for mental maths. Olli the owl brews potions: each bottle on the shelf holds a number and the cauldron shows the potion it needs. The child taps a bottle, a mixing spell (+, −, ×, ÷; German and French show sharing as a colon) and a second bottle, and the two pour together into a new bottle holding the result; the recipe so far is written down beside the shelf. Every bottle must be mixed in, and when the last one is exactly the cauldron's number the potion brews. Seven levels each teach one strategy on a trick card: plus or minus with two bottles, three bottles to 20, friends of ten, times potions, four bottles, sharing potions and a potion master level with all four spells. A round is six potions ending in a big cauldron with more bottles or a bigger potion that pays double. A potion brewed without a fizz wins three stars, after one fizz two, otherwise one; a wrong last bottle fizzes, and Olli pours that mix back so the child can try another way. Undo is always free, but a potion brewed without undoing earns a moon drop worth 10 coins, and three perfect potions in a row start a rainbow fever that doubles coins. After two fizzes Olli whispers the level's trick, never a recipe. The intro offers a remembered hourglass choice (No hourglass, Relaxed, Normal, Fast) whose time grows with the bottles; with an hourglass each potion carries a sparkle worth 15 coins that fades if the sand runs out, while the potion still brews. The scene is a 3D cauldron bubbling over a fire, with Olli on a stump and glass bottles on a shelf that lift when picked, pour into each other and into the cauldron, which turns gold and throws coins for a right potion and puffs grey smoke for a fizz, with a flat SVG kitchen as its fallback. Round stars come from the potion stars (17 of 18 earns three, 12 earns two), the best coins per level are remembered, every brewed potion warms the dragon egg, and all four languages include the game.

The Numbers world also offers The Firework Show (since November 2026), a subitizing game in the spirit of the Quick Images and Snapshots routines that research-based early maths curricula use. At a lakeside festival at dusk, Flick the fox fireworks-maker lights a rocket from the jetty; it bursts into a pattern of glowing sparks that holds for a moment and then fades, and the child says how many sparks there were without counting them one by one, by seeing the groups ("5 and 3 is 8", "a full ten and 4 is 14", "double 6 and one more is 13"). The spark pad shows the five-and-ten structure in rows of five, and answering is never timed: only the look is short. Twelve levels in five topics follow the arrangement order from the subitizing research: dice and small groups, five-structures, ten-frames and making ten from the empty spaces, two bursts side by side, a ten and more, mirror doubles, near doubles both ways, twenty-frames and a mixed grand show. Patterns are turned, mirrored and recoloured so no picture is memorised, and two bursts differ by place and spark shape, never colour alone. A right answer re-blooms the firework bigger with its groups outlined and the number sentence shown, and the child may answer "How did you see it?" (any honest answer, including "I counted", earns 5 spotter points and tunes the look time). A miss fizzles gently and the same pattern bursts again; after a second miss its groups are outlined and Flick asks a question about the structure ("Find the full row of five first", "Is the right one the same as the left one?"), never the count. A Peek button with a burning fuse re-bursts the pattern once per firework and costs only the first-look bonus. The level card shows how to play instead of telling it: a short film in the game's own pieces bursts a made-up ten-frame of 7 sparks, lets it fade, taps 7 on a little spark pad and re-blooms it with the five and the two outlined; a ? button replays the film during play, and for the first three fireworks on a device (and whenever a child waits six seconds) the whole spark pad or Next rocket hops with a gold halo, never a single key. The intro and a round fit one screen on phones and iPads, including a home-screen iPhone's insets, and the firework book sits under Start. The intro offers a remembered look pace (Gentle glow with free peeks, Glow, Pop) that adapts during the round. A round is twelve fireworks and a grand finale worth triple, announced with a banner, then every firework of the round goes up together in a closing show. Five first looks in a row start the Big show (gold trails, the crowd holds up lanterns, double points until a miss). Stars count first looks (10 of 12 earn three, 7 earn two, finishing earns one), the best points per level are remembered, and every level adds a firework shape (heart, star, flower, crown and more) to the child's firework book. The scene is a 3D dusk lake with a lantern-lit town, the crowd on the shore and Flick on the jetty, with a flat SVG sky as its fallback; bursts fade in and out and never strobe, and calm motion drops the rocket rise. Every right answer warms the dragon egg, and all four languages include the game.

The Tens world also offers The Star Catcher (since November 2026), the only game where the child places a number on a continuous line instead of picking or typing an answer, built on the linear number board research of Siegler and Ramani. Shooting stars fall at night over a fairy meadow, and the glowing bridge across it is a number line with its end posts labelled. Each star carries a number (later a sum, a difference or "halfway between 40 and 60"), and the child slides Nova the star keeper and her lantern net along the bridge, by dragging, tapping the bridge or with the arrow keys, then taps Catch. A catch inside the window drops the star into the lantern with a chime and points; a dead-on catch turns it into a golden star worth double, and Nova spins. A miss never loses the star: it bounces off the rim and floats back up, Nova first says only which way to go ("A bit further along!"), after a second miss a card asks a landmark question ("Is 63 more or less than 50, the middle?"), bringing back faded posts without numbers for that star, and from the third miss the net widens a little; nothing ever shows the spot or the value. Twelve levels in four topics teach landmarks instead of counting posts: numbers to 10, the middle, the bridge to 20, tens to 100, the squash zone with every ten labelled, neighbours either side of a ten, landmarks only in your head, sums to 20, tens sums, estimating two-digit sums, the bridge to 1000 and a mystery bridge whose far end is hidden. From level 5 about one star in five is another kind: where did it land, which is further, or a middle star. A round is ten stars (eight on sum levels) and a finale of three shooting stars worth double. Three first-try catches in a row light a comet trail on the lantern that doubles points until a miss. The intro offers a remembered pace (No rush, where stars hover until caught, or Relaxed, Normal and Fast); a falling star carries up to 15 points of stardust that drains as it falls, its trail shortening, and a star that reaches the bridge just hovers there without its bonus. Every caught star lights a point of the level's constellation (a heart, a castle, a crown, a unicorn, a dragon and more), and a finished picture stays in the sky as a collection shown on the intro and in later rounds. The scene is a 3D night sky with twinkling stars, a moon, the stone-and-glass bridge with lantern posts, Nova with fluttering wings and the falling stars, with a flat bridge as its fallback. Round stars come from first-try catches (8 of 10 earns three, 5 earns two), the best points per level are remembered, every caught star warms the dragon egg, and all four languages include the game.

The Dragon Hatchery (since November 2026) ties every game together, in the spirit of the pet-and-world rewards that keep children coming back to the best-selling maths apps. The child always carries one dragon egg: every right answer in the castle, the crocodile pond, the magic walls and the unicorn race, every chest opened in the treasure lock, every potion brewed in the cauldron and every star caught by Nova, warms it, and an egg chip in each game header fills up, glows with a +1 on every right answer and rocks gently once the egg is ready. The first egg needs 20 right answers (about two games), later ones 30, 40 and then 50. The Hatchery page (Dragons in the navigation, plus a card on Home) shows the egg in a 3D nest on a little floating meadow, glowing as it warms; a ready egg wobbles, cracks a little more with each of three taps and then pops its lid so a baby dragon grows out with a burst of sparkles. Six original baby dragons (Ember the fire dragon, Splash water, Fern leaf, Twinkle star, Puff cloud and Prism rainbow) hatch in random order, then six starlight versions sprinkled with glowing stars; after that eggs bring back old friends. The dragon book shows every dragon found as a 3D portrait, with eggs marked "?" for the ones still to hatch; tapping a dragon puts it in the nest and lets the child make it their favourite, who then waits for them on Home. The egg only ever adds; nothing is locked behind it. Everything stays on the device, and all four languages include the hatchery.

The games hub sorts every game's levels into topics (for example Adding, Taking away and Times tables in the castle), shown as a row of tabs above the level cards; the topic a child is working on opens by default, a link to a level opens its topic, and find-in-page still finds levels in other topics. The curriculum, the research on session length and the level map live in `docs/curriculum.md`.
